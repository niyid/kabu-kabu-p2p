Part 1 showed a serverless paradigm running in production. Part 2 asks the harder question: is I2P actually good enough — technically, competitively, and politically — to become the default substrate for how financial apps protect their users? The honest answer has caveats.

The Stakes

Every Breach Below Shared One Root Cause

2026 has been a brutal year for financial-sector security. Industry tracking shows finance-sector breach volume climbing sharply quarter over quarter, and the ransomware groups that once concentrated their effort on a handful of large banks have fragmented into dozens of smaller operators, each targeting the weakest vendor in a long, opaque supply chain.

48

32

Every incident in this report — and nearly every one described later in this article — traces back to the same structural fact: the data existed somewhere a determined attacker could eventually reach it. A central database, a vendor's cloud bucket, an offboarded employee's still-live credential, a third-party API with more access than it needed. I2P's relevance to finance isn't that it makes transactions faster or cheaper — it's that a serverless, garlic-routed architecture removes the single point that every one of these breaches depended on.

Root Causes

What's Actually Wrong With Financial Apps Today

Before I2P can be pitched as a fix, it's worth being precise about what's broken. Four patterns recur across nearly every major fintech and banking breach of the past two years.

1
Centralised data lakes as a single point of failure

Banks and fintechs still aggregate names, SSNs, account numbers, and transaction histories into large central stores because that's how relational architecture and regulatory reporting have always worked. One successful intrusion yields millions of complete identity profiles at once, rather than fragments.

2
Third-party and vendor sprawl

Modern financial products are assembled from a dozen vendors — data analytics firms, benefits administrators, cloud file-transfer tools, managed service providers. Each one is a new attack surface with access the end user never consented to and often doesn't know exists.

3
Offboarding and credential lifecycle failures

Departed employees or contractors retaining live access for months is a recurring vector — not a hypothetical one. Standing credentials with broad reporting access are a liability that persists long after the person who requested them has left.

4
Slow, compliance-driven disclosure

Breach notification windows measured in weeks, not hours, mean stolen identity data is already circulating before affected users can act. This isn't a technology failure by itself, but it compounds every other weakness on this list.

⚠️
The uncomfortable part

None of these four patterns are exotic zero-days. They're organisational and architectural defaults — the industry keeps building the same centralised target and then acting surprised when it's hit. A protocol-level fix only works if it's paired with a willingness to stop building that target in the first place.

Case Studies

Recent Breaches a Serverless I2P Architecture Would Have Defeated

These aren't hypothetical. Each of the following happened in the past eighteen months, and each one depended on the existence of a central, developer-controlled store of user data — exactly the thing Ghost Architecture eliminates by design.

Evolve Bank & Trust
Mid-2024 · LockBit ransomware

A ransomware group breached Evolve's systems and, after the bank refused to pay, published names, Social Security numbers, bank account numbers, dates of birth, and contact details. Because Evolve operated as banking-as-a-service infrastructure behind numerous fintech apps, the blast radius extended to customers who had never directly interacted with Evolve at all.

✓ Fix

In a serverless, on-device model, there is no equivalent central "banking-as-a-service" database for a ransomware group to encrypt and exfiltrate — the data was never aggregated upstream of the app in the first place.

Marquis (bank analytics vendor)
August 2025 · Ransomware

A data analytics vendor serving hundreds of banks was compromised, exposing names, addresses, card numbers, and Social Security numbers for over 672,000 people across multiple regions — none of whom had a direct relationship with Marquis, only with the banks that fed it their data.

✓ Fix

A third-party analytics vendor has nothing to analyse if raw customer data never leaves the device. Aggregate, privacy-preserving statistics could still be computed via local differential privacy without ever centralising raw records.

Figure Technology Solutions
Early 2026 · ShinyHunters

A blockchain-based lending platform disclosed a breach affecting close to a million customers, exposing names, dates of birth, addresses, phone numbers, and emails — a stark reminder that "blockchain-based" does not mean "serverless," and that on-chain settlement can still sit on top of a conventional, centrally-breachable customer database.

✓ Fix

This is the clearest illustration of the point: using a blockchain for settlement says nothing about where identity data lives. Pairing on-chain or Monero-style settlement with an I2P-routed, on-device identity layer closes exactly this gap.

Infosys McCamish / Allianz Life
Disclosed 2024–2025 · Class action settled 2025

A downstream processing vendor exposed names, addresses, dates of birth, and Social Security numbers for millions of insurance and brokerage customers, resulting in a roughly $17.5 million settlement covering 6.5 million affected people.

✓ Fix

Policy administration and claims processing don't inherently require a central copy of raw PII — selective disclosure via cryptographic proofs (see Section 5) can let a processor verify eligibility without ever holding the underlying record.

Block / Cash App offboarding failure
Disclosed 2022, litigated into 2025

A former employee retained the ability to pull reports on millions of brokerage customers months after leaving the company — exposing portfolio holdings and account numbers ideal for targeted social engineering, discovered only months after the fact.

✓ Fix

There is no internal reporting tool to misuse when there is no internal database to report from. Offboarding failures are a category of risk that a serverless architecture removes structurally rather than procedurally.

ℹ️
What I2P alone would not have stopped

Phishing-driven credential theft (National Bank of Asia) and employee social engineering still work against humans regardless of network architecture. I2P hardens the network layer; it does not replace security awareness training, hardware key-based MFA, or endpoint hygiene.

Protocol Analysis

I2P Today: What's Actually Strong, What Isn't Yet

I2P's core design choices are genuinely well-suited to financial-grade privacy, but "well-suited" is not the same as "finished." A fair audit has to hold both at once.

✅
Strength

Unidirectional garlic-routed tunnels

Inbound and outbound traffic travel different paths, and multiple messages are bundled into a single encrypted "garlic" — a structural defence against the traffic-correlation attacks that have historically de-anonymised Tor circuits.

✅
Strength

No central directory authorities

I2P's network map is maintained by a self-organising, rotating set of floodfill routers rather than a fixed set of directory authorities — removing the coordination points that represent a legal or technical pressure point in Tor's design.

✅
Strength

Deeper hop count by default

A full I2P round trip uses twelve hops (six inbound, six outbound) versus Tor's six-hop hidden-service circuit, producing stronger unlinkability at the cost of some latency — an entirely reasonable trade for financial workloads that are not latency-sensitive.

⚠️
Weakness

Small network, weaker anonymity set

I2P's active router count has grown from roughly 25,800 in early 2019 to about 72,600 by April 2025 — real growth, but still a small fraction of Tor's userbase. Anonymity networks get more anonymous as they get bigger; I2P's relative size is its biggest honest weakness.

⚠️
Weakness

Timing analysis is still possible

Garlic routing raises the cost of correlating sender and receiver, but a well-resourced adversary controlling enough entry and exit points can still compare message timing and size across a session. No overlay network fully solves this without heavy padding and latency cost.

🚧
Gap

No quantum-resistant cryptography yet

Current tunnel and message encryption relies on ElGamal/AES, which is not post-quantum secure. This matters specifically for financial data with decades-long confidentiality requirements, where "harvest now, decrypt later" is a realistic threat model.

The extensions that would matter most for finance

1
Post-quantum tunnel and signature crypto

The I2P project has an active proposal to adopt NIST FIPS 203 and 204 (ML-KEM / ML-DSA) across router identities, tunnel encryption, and stream/datagram signing, with a rollout targeted from late 2025 through mid-2027. For financial data with a multi-decade confidentiality horizon, this is not optional polish — it's a prerequisite for calling I2P finance-grade.

2
Garlic Routing V2 and tunnel-build hardening

Proposed extensions to the tunnel-build message format add certificate-based extensibility and reduce the metadata a hostile tunnel participant can infer, tightening the network's resistance to the centrality-based and Sybil-style attacks that academic research continues to probe.

3
Selective disclosure for compliance

Nothing in I2P itself provides KYC/AML-compatible selective disclosure. Financial apps built on it need a companion layer — zero-knowledge proofs or view-key-style mechanisms (as ScrowIt! and Àjọ already use on the Monero side) — so a regulator or auditor can verify a specific fact without the app operator holding a central copy of the underlying data.

4
Mobile-grade bootstrapping and battery profile

Embedding a full I2P router on a phone (as Kabu-Kabu and its siblings already do) is proof of concept, not yet a mature pattern with standard tooling. A finance-grade SDK needs first-class reseed reliability, background-execution limits handling, and documented battery/data-usage benchmarks before mainstream fintechs will adopt it.

Competitive Landscape

The honest verdict: Tor and I2P aren't really competing for the same job. Tor optimises for anonymously reaching the ordinary internet; I2P optimises for services that never need to leave the anonymous network at all. A financial app where two peers (or a peer and an escrow arbitrator) transact entirely within the app is precisely the workload I2P was designed for — which is why it's the better fit here, not because Tor is worse in general.

VPN-based privacy — trusts one provider, single jurisdiction
Zero-trust cloud — reduces but doesn't eliminate central data
Public blockchain settlement — transparent by default, needs a privacy layer
I2P + Monero — anonymised transport and private settlement, together

VPNs simply relocate trust to a single company and jurisdiction rather than removing it. Public blockchains solve custody but not privacy — a transparent ledger is a subpoena's best friend unless it's paired with something like Monero's ring signatures. "Zero-trust" cloud architecture is a genuine improvement over flat networks but still assumes a central data store exists; it minimises blast radius rather than eliminating the target. I2P is the only option on this list that removes the central store as a category.

The Path Forward

What It Would Actually Take to Become "The Standard"

Good technology doesn't become a de facto standard by being good — it becomes one by being easy to adopt, defensible to regulators, and boring enough that engineering teams choose it without a fight. I2P is closer to the first than the other two.

Now

Reference SDKs, not raw router jars

The Techducat ecosystem's SAM-bridge pattern — a plain TCP socket so app code never touches I2P internals — is the right shape. That pattern needs to become a published, versioned, documented SDK that any Android or iOS team can drop in without reading I2P's internal protocol specs first.

2025–27

Ship post-quantum crypto on schedule

The FIPS 203/204 rollout already in progress is the single highest-leverage protocol change for financial credibility. A network claiming to protect account data needs a credible answer to "what happens when today's ciphertext is harvested and decrypted in a decade" — and soon it will have one.

Ongoing

A compliance story that isn't "just trust us"

Regulators will not accept "we can't see the data" as a complete answer to AML and KYC obligations. The paradigm needs a standard selective-disclosure pattern — proofs of eligibility, sanctions-list, or age checks that don't require centralising the underlying record — built and documented once, then reused across every app in the ecosystem.

Ongoing

Independent security audits, published

Every app in the Techducat ecosystem making this claim needs a third-party audit trail, not just an architecture diagram. "Serverless by design" is a strong claim; it should be backed by the same rigour a bank would apply to a core banking vendor.

Longer term

Regulatory engagement, not regulatory avoidance

A privacy-by-architecture fintech sector will eventually need its own conversation with regulators — comparable to how the Travel Rule shaped crypto exchange compliance — rather than assuming anonymity alone is a durable strategy against a sector this heavily supervised.

🚫
What could stop this from ever becoming standard

Regulatory friction is the real risk, not technical failure. A network designed to make financial surveillance structurally impossible will draw scrutiny in exactly the jurisdictions with the strongest AML regimes. The paradigm's long-term viability depends as much on solving the compliance-without-custody problem as on any protocol improvement.

Additional Considerations

What Else Belongs in This Conversation

🔑
Key loss = fund loss

No password reset, ever

Non-custodial by design means there is no "forgot password" flow. A lost device or seed phrase is unrecoverable by construction. Any de facto standard needs a widely-adopted social or Shamir-style recovery pattern, or it will lose to custodial competitors on this alone.

📶
Bandwidth reality

I2P assumes a decent connection

Twelve-hop tunnels cost latency and data. In markets with constrained or metered mobile data — a real constraint in much of the Techducat team's own operating context — this is a genuine adoption barrier, not a footnote.

🏛️
Institutional inertia

Banks don't rewrite core systems casually

Incumbent financial institutions run systems with decades of compliance history behind them. I2P-based architecture is far more likely to win first among fintech challengers and the unbanked/underbanked segment than to displace core banking infrastructure directly.

🧪
Verifiability

Reproducible builds matter more here than anywhere

A privacy claim nobody can verify is just a marketing claim. F-Droid-style reproducible builds — already part of the Techducat pattern — need to become the norm, so users (and auditors) can confirm the shipped binary matches the published source.

Conclusion

Not a Silver Bullet — But the Right Direction

I2P will not single-handedly end fintech breaches. It doesn't stop phishing, doesn't fix offboarding processes by itself, and doesn't yet have a mature answer to post-quantum cryptography or regulatory-compliant selective disclosure. Those gaps are real and worth naming plainly rather than glossing over.

But look back at the five breaches in Section 3: Evolve, Marquis, Figure, Infosys McCamish, Block. Every single one depended on a central store of user data existing somewhere a developer, vendor, or attacker could reach it. That is precisely the assumption I2P-based, serverless architecture refuses to make. The protocol has real, documented weaknesses — but they're weaknesses of degree (network size, timing-analysis resistance, PQ readiness), not weaknesses of category. The industry's weaknesses are weaknesses of category: centralise first, secure later, hope the vendor didn't get breached.

A vault with no doors can't be broken into. It also can't be audited by looking through the keyhole. Getting both — privacy and verifiability — right is the actual unsolved problem.

— Article summary

The path to "de facto standard" runs through better tooling, on-schedule post-quantum crypto, a credible compliance story, and — most of all — enough independently-audited apps in production that the pattern stops looking experimental and starts looking obvious. Kabu-Kabu, ScrowIt!, and Àjọ are three data points. The next few years will show whether the rest of the industry treats them as a curiosity or a blueprint.
